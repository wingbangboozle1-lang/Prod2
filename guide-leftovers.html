How long are leftovers safe — and what does reheating actually require?

Three days or four? Fridge or freezer? And does the appliance you cooked in have any say in the answer? The USDA factsheet on leftovers answers all of it, and the air-fryer part is more interesting than the advice usually given.

The short answer

Refrigerate within two hours of cooking. Eat refrigerated leftovers within 3 to 4 days, or freeze them for 3 to 4 months. Reheat to 165°F, measured with a food thermometer — and bring sauces, soups and gravies to a rolling boil. Every figure here is from the USDA FSIS factsheet Leftovers and Food Safety (page dated "Last Updated: Jul 31, 2020"; read 2026-11-20) (S-FSIS-LEFTOVERS).

The clock, in the order you meet it

  1. Two hours out, not four. "Within 2 hours of cooking food or after it is removed from an appliance keeping it warm, leftovers must be refrigerated," the factsheet says — and perishable food left out longer than that is food to throw away, with the window cut to one hour if the room or the picnic is above 90°F (S-FSIS-LEFTOVERS, read 2026-11-20).
  2. Cool it fast, not politely. Divide large amounts into shallow containers; slice whole roasts and hams; break a turkey into pieces. The factsheet is explicit that a big pot of soup cooling slowly is the hazard, and that hot food can go straight into the refrigerator or be chilled in an ice or cold-water bath first (S-FSIS-LEFTOVERS).
  3. Wrap it, then date it. Cover, wrap airtight, or seal in a container — the stated reasons are keeping bacteria out, keeping moisture in, and keeping the fridge odour out of the food (S-FSIS-LEFTOVERS).
  4. Reheat to a number, not to a smell. 165°F as measured with a food thermometer, cover the food while it reheats, and bring liquids to a rolling boil (S-FSIS-LEFTOVERS). The Danger Zone page puts the same rule as "reheated thoroughly to an internal temperature of 165°F or until hot and steaming" (S-FSIS-DANGER).

The 3–4 day figure is also what the peer-reviewed Nebraska Extension air-fryer handout uses in its own leftover instruction — "Store leftovers in a sealed container in the refrigerator for up to four days" (S-UNL-AIRFRYER, updated 2025, read 2026-11-20). Two independent sources, same window.

Where the air fryer fits — and where the guidance goes quiet

The factsheet names three appliances for reheating: a saucepan, a microwave, and an oven. It also says it is safe to reheat frozen leftovers without thawing "in the oven or microwave (for example, casseroles and combination meals)" (S-FSIS-LEFTOVERS). It does not name an air fryer. That silence matters for how you read every "reheat leftovers in the air fryer at 350°F for 4 minutes" listicle on the internet: the number is not derived from the guidance, and this page will not invent one either.

What can be said from the sources is narrower but real:

  • An air fryer is a small convection oven. The extension description is explicit: air fryers "cook by circulating extremely hot air around the food (also known as convection cooking)" with a heating element above the food and a fan moving it (S-UNL-AIRFRYER). Whether that makes the factsheet's oven instruction apply to your countertop unit is a reading, not a quotation — so treat it as the question to ask, not the answer.
  • The failure mode is the same one that ruins a fresh batch. "Caution should be used to not overfill the basket or tray as foods will not cook evenly or reach the proper temperature" (S-UNL-AIRFRYER). A heaped basket of rice or a pressed-down layer of fries is exactly the situation where the centre stays cold while the edges get crisp — and the only way to know which one you have is the thermometer.
  • Cleaning is part of the leftover workflow. The same page's instruction is blunt: "It is important to properly clean your air fryer basket/tray after each use." That is also the reason to weigh the dishwasher-safe claims on a listing before you buy, which is what the cleanup and counter-space guide is for.
  • The manual is where an appliance-specific answer would live. The extension tells readers to "check out the operator's manual to learn more about how the air fryer works." This site has not read the manuals for the three units it follows, so it publishes no reheating time or temperature for them — and the register stays blank rather than filling the gap from a guess (Q-P18).

No cooking performance is claimed here. What follows is the listed container geometry, because leftover reheating is a surface-area problem and capacity is the only geometry the register carries:

  • Two dishes that finish together, in different amounts. P1 lists two independent 3 qt baskets with Sync Finish and separate controls — useful precisely when one container holds a gratin and the other holds something that needs crisping. Specs: P1.
  • One big single basket, spread out. P3 lists an 8 qt capacity with an XL nonstick basket described as dishwasher-safe, and a 4-in-1 function set that includes a frozen setting. A single 8 qt basket is the flat-layer answer of the three. Specs: P3.

Questions this page answers

Is it four days or seven?

Three to four days in the refrigerator, per the USDA factsheet quoted above. The "up to four days" phrasing in the extension handout matches it. Any longer window you have read comes from someone else's rule of thumb, not from this guidance.

Can I put a hot casserole straight in the fridge?

Yes — and the guidance prefers speed over the old "let it cool completely first" habit. It states that hot food can be placed directly in the refrigerator or rapidly chilled in an ice or cold water bath, and that dividing large portions into shallow containers is what actually protects the food.

Do I have to thaw frozen leftovers before reheating?

No. The factsheet says it is safe to reheat frozen leftovers without thawing, in a saucepan, microwave, or oven, and warns only that it takes longer. If you thaw in the microwave instead, the instruction is to heat to 165°F as measured with a thermometer.

Can I refreeze what gets left over twice?

Yes, on two conditions the page draws explicitly: either the food was reheated to 165°F before you refreeze it, or you thawed it in the refrigerator, took the portion you needed, and put the remainder back without reheating. The quality caveat is the same one as the freezer clock — texture and flavour, not safety.

Does a smell test count?

No. The chart page this guide leans on states the reason directly: you cannot see, smell or taste harmful bacteria. Reheat to 165°F or do not serve it.
