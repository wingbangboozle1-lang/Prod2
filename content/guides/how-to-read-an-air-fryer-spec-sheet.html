A spec sheet is a menu of true statements arranged so the flattering ones are easy to find. Nothing on it is a lie - but the numbers you care about are rarely the numbers printed largest. Here is how to read one in order, from the lines that matter most to the fine print that quietly decides whether you'll like the machine.

Line 1: capacity, converted and doubted

Find the volume (quarts or litres) and convert to one unit: 1 quart = 0.946 litres. Then remember what it measures - total drawer volume, not food area. On our register, the single-basket Chefman lists 8 quarts (about 7.6 litres) while the dual-basket model lists 6 litres total across two 3-quart baskets (official listings, read 2026-11-16). Same kitchen job, very different numbers - which is why the capacity checklist exists.

Line 2: dimensions, all three of them

Footprint is the first spec to rule a machine out, and depth is the number that rules out the most. Listings print depth first, then width, then height. Our three verified examples, exactly as listed: 13.38 D × 11.81 W × 10.63 H (dual basket), 16.9 D × 15.8 W × 14.8 H (French-door oven), 12.75 D × 13.5 W × 10 H (single basket). Compare depth against your counter, height against your shelf, and remember the manual's clearance requirements sit on top of all three.

Line 3: wattage, read honestly

Wattage is heat-up speed and recovery between batches, not "power" over your food's quality. Anything in the typical countertop range preheats within minutes; what wattage really decides is whether a second batch starts hot. It also decides plugs: a high-wattage machine sharing a circuit with a microwave can trip a breaker mid-dinner. Check what else lives on that outlet.

Line 4: temperature range, stated plainly

Look for the top temperature (higher sears better - one of our register's listings advertises a 450°F Hi-Fry mode, as listed) and the bottom (dehydrate-style low-and-slow needs one). Range is a real spec. Presets, by contrast, are marketing: a "fries" preset is time plus temperature plus a fan curve, and every owner abandons presets within a month for manual settings anyway.

The fine print that decides satisfaction

  • Cord length. Counter machines sit far from outlets; short cords are a safety choice, not an oversight. A short cord plus a clearance rule can make your ideal spot impossible.
  • Clearance requirements. The manual's required air gap around the machine - check it before choosing the spot, not after the first shut-off.
  • "Digital" versus dial. Touch panels are fine until wet hands meet flat glass; dials fail less but guess less. Neither is a spec - it's a preference the listing can't make for you.
  • Materials of the washable parts. Dishwasher-safe basket is a listed feature on two of our three verified machines; the chamber and element never are.
  • What's in the box. Accessories listed here are the cheapest to evaluate - anything that blocks airflow or adds scrubbing is a cost dressed as a gift.

The one-line rule

Read a spec sheet in this order: usable capacity, depth, wattage, temperature range, then everything else. If a machine survives that order against your kitchen's measurements, the lifestyle photos can't hurt you. The comparison table arranges the three verified listings in exactly this order.
