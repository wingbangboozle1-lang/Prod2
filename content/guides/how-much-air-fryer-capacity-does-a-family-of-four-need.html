For a family of four, the honest answer is: more than the box suggests, less than the biggest machine implies - because the variable that matters isn't capacity, it's batches. Run the math for a real weeknight and the size question answers itself.

The batch math

Air fryer cooking is single-layer cooking. Food needs air underneath it to brown, so capacity only counts while it fits one layer. One serving of the usual weeknight suspects - fries, nuggets, veg, a fillet - wants roughly a hand-span of single-layer area. A family plate of four therefore means either one generous layer or two batches.

Translate that into the labels: a 5-to-6-quart drawer holds a single layer suited to two servings, so a family of four cooks two short batches - workable, invisible on a Tuesday, tedious three nights running. An 8-quart square basket (like the TurboFry on our register, as listed) widens the layer enough that many family meals fit one batch. A 6-litre dual-basket machine (also on our register, two independent 3-quart baskets) runs two foods in two drawers at two temperatures, finishing together - batch time, but not batch waits. And a 24.6-litre French-door oven (the third machine on our register) takes a family plate on one tray, at the price of the deepest body on the counter. What "serves a family" on the box actually means

Box math assumes the smallest plausible serving. A "family size" claim that fits four portions of fries won't fit four portions of drumsticks, and drumsticks are what Thursday actually cooks. When a listing says "serves X," read it as "serves X portions of the lightest thing you might cook." Your real portion - the chicken thighs, the tray of vegetables - is roughly half that.

When two batches beat one big basket

Big-basket cooks pay for their capacity every time they wash up and every time they clear counter space. Two batches in a modest basket costs about five extra minutes. If your meals are tolerant (fries while the fillet rests), two batches win. If your meals need everything done together (a plate where the sides go cold), they don't - and either a wide single basket or a dual-basket setup earns its price.

The decision, in one pass

  1. Name your Thursday. The meal you cook most decides the batch count.
  2. One batch preferred? Shop wide single layers (8-quart class) or oven trays (24-litre class), and measure counter depth before ordering - the counter-space method makes the comparison honest.
  3. Two foods, two temperatures, one finish time? That's the dual-basket pitch, and it's real: two independent drawers with a sync function (as listed on our register's P1).
  4. Everything else? A 5-to-6-quart drawer plus a willingness to run two batches covers a family of four without dominating the counter.
