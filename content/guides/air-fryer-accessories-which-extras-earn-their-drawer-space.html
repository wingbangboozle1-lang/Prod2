The accessory aisle exists to solve one problem - getting food and air closer together - and most of its products solve the opposite. Two questions sort the entire shelf: does it improve airflow, and does it improve batch size? Everything else is drawer clutter.

Accessories that earn their space

  • A pump oil sprayer. Not a propellant can - a refillable pump mister. It puts a thin, even film on food without the propellant additives that damage nonstick coatings, and it's the only accessory that improves nearly every cook. That coating, by the way, is exactly why: the dishwasher-safe baskets on our register's two Chefman machines (as listed) are nonstick, and nonstick's enemy is harsh spray propellant as much as metal forks.
  • An extra rack (the right height). A second layer genuinely improves batch size - provided your machine's height allows air to move above the top layer. Measure the cavity, not the rack.
  • Silicone cups, for the egg-and-reheat jobs. They hold small, saucy or delicate things the basket can't. They don't crisp anything (airflow can't reach the food), so they're a tool for specific jobs, not a daily driver.
  • A soaking tub or dedicated soak brush. Unglamorous, and the only accessory that reliably shortens the cleanup this site's cleanup guide describes. Burnt-on residue on a crumb tray soaks off; scorched onto a coating, it never leaves.

Accessories that usually don't

  • Solid parchment liners. The popular ones. A solid sheet at the basket's floor blocks the airflow that makes the machine work, which is why liner instructions tell you to add food or weigh the sheet down before preheating - a hot, light liner can lift onto the element. Perforated liners exist for a reason, but honestly: most baskets don't need any liner, and the basket washes easier than the debate deserves.
  • Tall stackers. Three stacked grids in a 6-inch cavity is a photo, not a cooking arrangement. Airflow dies at layer two unless the machine was designed for it (oven-style machines with rack height to spare are the exception).
  • The gadget peelers, corers, pizza pans. Each solves a job your oven or knife already solves, and each takes drawer space. Skip until a specific recipe demands one twice.

The multi-function overlap

Multi-function machines often ship with the accessories that justify their functions - rotisserie spits, extra racks, crumb trays. Before buying extras, inventory what's in the box: the multi-function guide walks through exactly that. What's in the box is listed on the listing page; it's the cheapest place to shop.

The one-line rule

Buy accessories that move air or add layers. Skip anything that blocks airflow, regardless of the photos. And when a product page shows the machine loaded with accessories, remember: that's a styling shot, not a cooking arrangement - the spec sheet and the comparison table are where the truth lives.
