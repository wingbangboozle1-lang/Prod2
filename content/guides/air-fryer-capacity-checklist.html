Capacity labels are marketing territory. "Serves a family of five," "XXL," "8-in-1 family size" - none of it is a spec. The spec is the volume in quarts or litres, and even that lies by omission, because what actually limits dinner is the flat area where food touches the basket in one layer. Here is the checklist that survives contact with a real kitchen.

1. Convert the label, then ignore it

Quarts and litres measure the drawer's volume, including the air above the food line. A "6-quart" basket and a "6-litre" basket are different machines (6 quarts is about 5.7 litres). Write the number in one unit before comparing anything. On this site's register: the dual-basket Chefman lists 6 litres, the single-basket TurboFry lists 8 quarts, the French-door oven lists 24.6 litres - all read from their official listing pages on 2026-11-16.

2. Ask what the shape holds, not the number

Volume above the max-fill line is marketing volume. The honest question: how many chicken thighs, or how many fries, fit in one layer? Round baskets waste corner space; square baskets don't. Dual baskets trade one big layer for two independent smaller ones - useful when the two foods need different temperatures, wasteful when they don't. An oven's layers only help if your foods don't need shaking, because racks block the drawer-shake ritual.

3. Check the height, not just the floor

Anything tall - a whole chicken, a layered casserole, a rack of stuffed peppers - needs vertical clearance under the heating element. A squat, wide drawer that crams a 4-pound bird will steam the top and scorch the skin. If whole birds are on your menu, note the listed height of the cooking cavity and be sceptical of every listing photo showing food touching the element.

4. Do the two-batch math

Most weeknight cooking in an air fryer is batches, and that's fine. The real capacity question is how many batches your meal can tolerate: two short batches for fries-and-nuggets is invisible; four batches for a full family plate turns a 20-minute dinner into an hour. A rough rule from real kitchens: one serving of mixed mains-and-sides wants roughly a hand-span of single-layer area. Four servings means either one large layer or two batches - a 6-to-8 quart drawer usually lands on two batches, and a 24-litre oven does it in one. Our family-of-four guide runs the full math.

5. Price the storage, not just the counter

Capacity you don't cook with, you store. Big-capacity machines get moved in and out of cupboards, and their weight - often unlisted - becomes a daily tax. If the machine will live in a cupboard, capacity per kilogram of lifting matters more than headline volume.

The one-line rule

Buy for the meal you cook most, not the biggest number on the box. A modest basket you use every night beats a family oven that lives in the cupboard because moving it felt like a chore by Thursday.
