Every air fryer charges two costs the listing never prints: what it takes to clean after each cook, and what it takes to live with it on the counter. Buyers pay the cleanup cost weekly and the footprint cost daily, and they almost always underestimate both. Here is how to price them in ten minutes before you buy.

The cleanup cost: count the parts, then count the crevices

Start with the parts list. A basket air fryer is two washable parts - basket and crumb tray - plus a wipe-down of the chamber. Oven-style machines add racks, a rotating crumb shelf, a door with glass and, on rotisserie models, spit and forks. More parts isn't automatically worse (a big oven can be easier than scrubbing three basket batches), but the count is knowable before purchase, so know it.

Then the crevices. Nonstick baskets are easy until something burns onto the heating element above them, which no basket soaking reaches. Dishwasher claims help: the dual-basket Chefman and the 8-quart TurboFry on our register both list dishwasher-safe baskets (official listings, read 2026-11-16) - but dishwasher-safe covers the removable parts, never the chamber or the element above it. If you hate scrubbing, nonstick coating condition matters more over time than any single feature: metal utensils and abrasive pads are what kill it.

The footprint cost: depth first, height second, weight always

Counter depth is the constraint kitchens forget. Most counters run about 60-65 cm before the backsplash, and manufacturers require clearance behind and around the machine - the exact figures live in each manual, so check them rather than trusting us. A deep body that must float away from the wall eats usable prep space twice: once for itself, once for its clearance.

Height matters on shelves and under cabinets; depth matters on counters; weight matters every time you move the thing. If the machine will live in a cupboard, weight and a bulk-heavy shape are a daily tax. If it lives on the counter, depth is. Our capacity-per-centimetre method turns the depth question into one comparable number, and the five-minute measuring routine turns it into a checklist for your actual kitchen.

The trade-off test

  1. Will it live on the counter or in a cupboard? Counter: optimize depth and finish (matte hides fingerprints; glass doors show grease). Cupboard: optimize weight, handle bulk and cord storage.
  2. Who washes, and how often? Daily washers should minimize parts and prefer dishwasher-safe baskets. Weekend cooks can tolerate an oven's parts list in exchange for its range.
  3. What's above and behind the spot? Heat-clearance rules from the manual decide whether the "free corner" is real.

One honest warning: this page can't tell you which machine is easiest to live with, because that depends on your sink, your counter and your week - not on any spec sheet.
