Multi-function appliances sell on imagination: you, using all 25 functions, weekly. Reality runs differently - most owners settle into two or three functions, and the rest are buttons. The questions below separate the machine you'll use from the machine you'll advertise to guests.

1. Which functions will you use in an ordinary week?

Name three meals from your actual last two weeks. If air-fry, toast and bake cover them, a dedicated air fryer plus your existing oven may beat a combo machine - and costs you less counter depth. If rotisserie or dehydrate made your list honestly, the multi-function case is real.

2. Does the function count hide a discrepancy?

Function counts are marketing arithmetic - presets that are just time-and-temperature pairs get counted individually. And sometimes the arithmetic doesn't even agree with itself: the French-door oven on our register is titled with 24 cooking functions in its listing title, while the register's observation of its panel counted 25 (official listing, read 2026-11-16). Neither number tells you what the machine does well. Count the functions you'd use, not the number on the box.

3. What does each function cost in cleanup?

Rotisserie means spit, forks and drippings. Dehydrate means hours of low heat and a sticky tray. Toast in an air-fryer oven means crumb management that drawers never face. A function you'd use weekly is cheap; a function you'd use monthly still charges you its cleanup every time.

4. Does multi-function cost capacity where you need it?

Combo ovens are big, but their racks and trays divide a cavity. A 26-quart body can hold one large sheet-pan meal or several small ones - not both at once. If your bottleneck is single-layer area for a family plate, check what a layer holds, not what the box holds.

5. Are the controls legible at 7 a.m.?

Twenty-five functions need a control panel, and control panels get complicated. Look for the two or three functions you named in question one: are they one press away, or buried in a menu cycle? Photos of the panel are in every listing; read the panel, not the lifestyle shot.

6. What breaks first, and who fixes it?

More mechanisms, more failure points: fans, door hinges, rotisserie motors, element zones. The listing won't answer this one - the warranty length and the manufacturer's parts policy will, and both are checkable before purchase. A drawer air fryer has almost nothing to break beyond an element and a fan; price that simplicity honestly.

7. What does it replace - and what does it relocate?

The honest multi-function math is subtraction: if the oven replaces your toaster and your second oven, it earns its footprint twice. If it replaces nothing because the toaster is faster before school and the big oven handles Sunday, it's a single-function machine with a reputation.

The one-line rule

Buy functions you already know you need, in a body your counter has already measured. The comparison table shows the multi-function option next to the two single-purpose formats so you can weigh them side by side.
