How accurate does a meat thermometer need to be?

±0.5°F, ±1°F, NIST-certified: what the accuracy claims on these listings are actually promising — and which thermometer fits which kind of cook.

Food safety and medium-rare both come down to a few degrees in the thickest part of the protein. That's why accuracy claims lead thermometer listings. But the claim is only half the story; the workflow — instant spot-check versus continuous monitoring — decides which machine you'll actually reach for.

What ±0.5°F and ±1°F mean

The two thermometers on this site state their tolerance plainly:

  • P4 — "±0.5℉ Accuracy" with a NIST certification claimed in the listing title, and a thermocouple sensor with a 1-second read (read from its listing, 2026-11-19).
  • P5 — "Accurate within 1˚F" as listed in its product summary, across three leave-in probes (same read date).

For doneness purposes, ±1°F is tight — the difference between 145°F and 146°F is not something a palate arbitrates. The ±0.5°F + NIST claim on P4 is the stricter promise: NIST-traceable calibration is a documented chain of comparison against a national standard, as the listing frames it. (We record the claim as listed; the certificate itself is not published on the listing — so treat "NIST-certified" as the listing's stated attribute, flagged for what it doesn't show.)

First, what are you trying to hit?

Accuracy only means something against a target. These are the official safe minimum internal temperatures published by USDA and FoodSafety.gov — read on 2026-11-20 from the page last reviewed 2024-11-21. They are the reference points a thermometer reading is being judged against.

Read the gaps in that table and the accuracy question answers itself. Between 145°F and 160°F there is a 15-degree gap; a thermometer that is accurate to ±1°F resolves it with room to spare. Where accuracy earns its price is at the thin margin you personally care about — a 137°F medium-rare versus a 142°F medium — and in how fast you get the number before the meat carries over past it.

The cold side matters too

A thermometer is not only for the cook. USDA publishes the storage limits that decide whether the leftovers are still good: refrigeration at 40°F (4°C) or below, freezing at 0°F (-18°C) or below, and — after a power cut — a refrigerator that holds safe temperature for about four hours if the door stays shut, with perishables discarded after four hours without power (S-USDA-COLD, last reviewed 2023-09-19; S-USDA-OUTAGE, last reviewed 2024-08-08; both read 2026-11-20). An instant-read is how you check the fridge is actually doing its job.

Instant-read versus leave-in wireless

This is the real fork:

  • Instant-read (P4): open, poke, read in a listed second, withdraw. Ideal for grills, roasts checked near the end, frying oil, candy stages. It cannot watch a roast while you're outside.
  • Wireless leave-in (P5): probes stay in from the start; a hub and app relay live temperatures and alerts. Listed: 1000°F heat-safe probes, 70+ hours of probe use per charge, air-fryer-safe probes. Ideal for long cooks, smoking, and anyone who wants to stop opening the oven.

Many serious kitchens own both — the instant-read for the last two minutes, the leave-in for the first two hours. If you own neither, decide which minute of cooking worries you more.

What to check on any thermometer listing

  1. Stated tolerance (±0.5°F, ±1°F) — and whether a certification claim backs it.
  2. Response time — instant-reads claim 1–3 seconds; that's the poke-to-reading time.
  3. Probe heat limit — leave-in probes state a max (P5: 1000°F as listed); instant-reads are not left in.
  4. Waterproofing and cleaning — P4 states IP67 in its title (with its own subtitle inconsistency); P5 lists probes as waterproof and dishwasher safe.
  5. Variant count — P5 is a 1/2/3/4-probe family; the record here is the 3-probe Gen3.

The one-sentence answer

Accuracy within ±1°F is enough for doneness; the choice that matters is whether you want to check the temperature (instant-read) or watch it (leave-in wireless). Everything else is features on those two themes.
