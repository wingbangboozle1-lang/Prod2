What temperature counts as done? The chart, food by food

The number that decides whether dinner worked is not a watt, a quart or a preset count — it is measured inside the food. Here is the official chart, what the rest times actually apply to, and where each of the two thermometers we follow earns its place.

The short answer

All poultry and leftovers: 165°F. Ground meats: 160°F. Whole-muscle beef, pork, veal and lamb, plus fish and shellfish: 145°F with at least three minutes of rest. Eggs: 160°F. Every one of those is a minimum measured with a food thermometer, taken from the USDA FSIS safe minimum internal temperature chart (last updated Apr 14, 2025; read 2026-11-20) (S-FSIS-CHART).

The chart, as published

Reproduced from the FSIS page rather than paraphrased, because the details that get lost in paraphrase are the ones that matter — the rest time on some rows and not others, and the separate instruction for a fully cooked ham.

Three things the chart says that a product page never will

1. The reason a thermometer is not optional

The chart opens with the sentence that justifies the whole appliance category: "You can't see, smell or taste harmful bacteria that may cause illness" (S-FSIS-CHART, read 2026-11-20). Colour, texture and juice clarity are heuristics the official guidance explicitly declines to rely on. That is the honest argument for owning a thermometer — not a brand claim.

2. Rest time belongs to specific rows, not to everything

The three-minute rest is printed on the whole-muscle beef/pork/veal/lamb rows and on fresh or smoked ham. It is not printed on the ground-meat, poultry, egg, fish, leftover or casserole rows. Treating "rest 3 minutes" as a universal rule is a common paraphrase error; the chart is narrower than that, and carries the four standing guidelines alongside it — Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill.

3. It is a floor, not a target

The same page says consumers "may choose to cook food to higher temperatures" for reasons of personal preference (S-FSIS-CHART). A 165°F breast is safe; a 172°F breast is also safe and might be drier. The chart is the line below which the guidance will not go, and nothing here argues you should stop at it.

What changes in an air fryer — and what does not

Nothing in the chart is appliance-specific, and the peer-reviewed Nebraska Extension guidance on air fryers says the same thing the government page does: "Using a food thermometer is the only way to ensure safety of meat, poultry, and egg products when air frying", with the probe placed in the thickest part and kept off bone, fat and gristle (S-UNL-AIRFRYER, peer-reviewed, updated 2025, read 2026-11-20).

The one air-fryer-specific risk is the opposite of a temperature problem: it is a loading problem. The same extension page warns against overfilling the basket because "foods will not cook evenly or reach the proper temperature." That sentence is the bridge between this page and the capacity checklist: the appliance that is too small for the batch is also the appliance that misses the target.

Two details we noticed while reading, and are not smoothing over. The extension's own temperature table prints the egg row as "160°F (73.9°C)", while the FSIS chart prints 160°F with 71.1°C; 160°F is the figure both sources agree on, so that is what this page publishes, and the conversion discrepancy is on the record at F-P31. And the extension's table has no leftovers row at all — the 165°F leftover standard on this page comes from the FSIS chart.

Questions this page answers

Is 165°F only about chicken?

No. The FSIS chart puts all poultry, ground poultry, leftovers, casseroles and most reheated ham at 165°F. Beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts sit lower, at 145°F plus a three-minute rest, because the surface is where the contamination is on an intact cut; ground meat moves surface material through the whole batch, which is why it is at 160°F.

Does an air fryer need a higher temperature setting to reach these numbers?

The chart sets no air-fryer-specific oven temperature, so there is no honest "set it to X" answer to give. What the sources do say is that an overfilled air fryer stops reaching the proper temperature, and that the internal reading is the only reliable check. This site publishes no cook times because none of the listings we read states a time-and-temperature table for a specific food.

Can I tell that a roast is done without a thermometer?

Not safely, per the guidance quoted above: harmful bacteria cannot be seen, smelled or tasted. Timers, colour and " juices running clear" are heuristics the official chart explicitly does not substitute for a reading.

Do I need a second thermometer for the air fryer?

No. The standard is a temperature, not an instrument, and the same instant-read works at the basket, in the oven and on the grill. Where a second device earns its place is the leave-in case: a long cook you want to walk away from. That is a workflow argument, not a safety requirement.
